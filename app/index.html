The Level Field

The AFL ladder ranks 18 clubs that never played the same season. Everyone gets six opponents twice, and those six are picked for broadcast. Travel is decided by geography. Goal kicking, which barely repeats, is baked into every result. Strip all three out and the year looks different.

Level the field and two clubs in the 2026 finals eight do not belong there.

How to read your club

Every club already pays for a rating system, and those answer who is best. The question here is one nobody publishes: how much of the ladder was settled before round one?

If your club climbs on earned, the ball was unkind to it. Expect some of that to come back next year on its own.

Twenty-three rounds, 17 opponents. The arithmetic forces every club to play six teams twice, and which six comes down to what broadcasts well. Clubs complain about it every October. The league says the draw is fair. Neither side has ever put a number on it. Here is one.

What the fixture is actually worth

Two beliefs this data does not back up. Clubs raise the six-day break every season. Once you control for who is playing whom, it is worth nothing you can measure. The bigger surprise is home ground advantage, which mostly vanishes once travel is pulled out of it. Ten Victorian clubs share Melbourne venues, so hosting a cross-town rival buys you very little. What actually costs the visitor is the flight, not the crowd.

Why this number

Watch what the model refuses to believe. It will not accept that a club is simply a good kick for goal. A side shooting well above the league average keeps only about a quarter of that edge in its forecast, since that is all that survives into the next half of a season. Chance creation is a different story. That it takes almost at face value.

The metrics, in plain words

and how much repeats
How “how much repeats” is measured. Split every club’s season into odd and even matches, work the number out twice, and see whether the two halves agree. If they do, the number is measuring the team. If they do not, it is measuring the weather. Across 2015–2026 that is 216 club-seasons, and it is the single most useful thing in this whole project.

How accurate is any of this?

tested on matches it never saw

So there is real signal here, and the model is also clearly worse in September. Use the finals figure when you talk about finals. Three things drive the gap, and only the last one is fixable. Finals involve the best sides alone, so there is less to get right. The model rarely commits past 75%, so it collects little credit when it calls a blowout. And it knows nothing about a week off, or a knockout played interstate.

What is not here

Ask a player how games are won and you will hear about contested ball, pressure, territory and structure. They are right. None of it is in this data. The feed gives you goals, behinds, scores, the venue, the date and two team names. Nothing else.

No disposals, then. No inside 50s, no clearances, no tackle counts, no contested possession, no player records, no injury lists, no quarter-by-quarter scores. Any metric here claiming to measure those would be made up.

Scoring shots do capture the end product of all that work. You never see the tackle, but you see whether the chain finished with a shot at goal. On a public feed that is as far as anyone can honestly go, and it gets you most of the way.

Match results come from the Squiggle API, the same underlying source as AFL Tables. Every figure on this site is rebuilt from that feed, so the numbers move as the season does.
